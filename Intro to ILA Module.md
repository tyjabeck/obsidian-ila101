### Term Life

The idea behind term life insurance is that the need for death benefit protection is fleeting or temporary. Term life insurance is temporary coverage that provides a financial safety net for someone’s dependents over a time period between one and 30 years.

Advantages
- Possibly the least expensive consumer option.
- Provides a large death benefit for the amount of premium paid when there are no cash values.
- Highly competitive market allows consumers to shop for the best rates.
- Offers multiple risk classes and placement into a class is determined by underwriting.
- Many individual term policies will provide conversion options to permanent life insurance without the need for underwriting.
- Can be developed to meet a number of different temporary needs.
- Riders can offer supplemental coverage for a variety of needs.

Disadvantages
- If the contract does not have a return of premium feature, there is a "use it or lose it" aspect.
- Increasing premium structures may result in the policy becoming unaffordable.
- Death benefits cannot be increased without additional underwriting after the policy has been issued.
- From the writing company's perspective, the initial strain associated with acquisition is high compared to the premium collected. Principle-based reserving (PBR) helps to reduce initial strain, and there are other risk mitigation aspects, too.

### Whole Life

Whole life insurance provides death benefit protection for the life of the insured. Should the insured live past the maturity date of the policy, the cash value at that point equals the death benefit and is paid to the owner. Unlike the death benefit, a maturity benefit payment may be taxable if there is a gain in the policy at the time of the payment.

Advantages
- Usually is structured to have guaranteed premiums which are level for the entire premium payment period.
- Guaranteed development of cash value provides an opportunity to use the policy as collateral for a loan, or you can take loans from the insurance policy.
- If you outlive the life insurance policy, a maturity benefit is paid, which is equal to the death benefit.
- If you surrender the policy prior to maturity, a cash surrender value may be paid.

Disadvantages
- It is more expensive than term insurance.
- The provider has increased exposure to investment yield risk.
- Less death benefit protection per $1,000 of premium when compared to term insurance.
- Increased administrative cost compared to term insurance.

### Universal Life

Universal life insurance is permanent life insurance with an investment savings element and low premiums like term life insurance. Universal life insurance is an unbundled life insurance where the premiums can be either fixed or flexible. The premiums paid are deposited into a policy account and charges are taken from the account periodically. Earnings on the account are credited and usually offer a nominal guarantee that the account will not decrease due to investment performance. The policy’s account value may decrease due to the charges withdrawn from the account.

Advantages
- The design creates a lot of flexibility to meet different needs.
- The credited interest to the policy’s account value has the potential to mirror investment market returns.
- Different designs have led to newer products, which will be discussed in greater detail in the next few slides.

Disadvantages 
- Complexity leads to confusion for all parties.
- Greater care is needed in the administration because of state and federal requirements.
- Misleading and inaccurate disclosures in the past have led to class action lawsuits (e.g., Vanishing Premium).
- The return on the policy has the potential to be less than market returns because of the charges subtracted from the account.
- If the policy’s account is depleted, the policy may terminate.

### Variable Universal Life

In the late 1980s, Universal Life (UL) Insurance evolved where much of the insurance risk remained with the company, and the interest rate risk shifted to the policyowner. Under a Variable Universal Life design, the policyowner could elect separate accounts in which the account value of the life policy is invested. These separate accounts act similar to a mutual fund where the policyowner can elect a different type of account based on their risk tolerance and desired return expectations.

Advantages
- Investment gains can exceed those of the portfolio assets of a life insurance company.
- The investment orientation requires that a suitability analysis be conducted by a Principal to ensure the risk is appropriate for the policyowner.
- The separate accounts were often sold with rebalancing and dollar cost averaging strategies to help individuals manage their risk tolerance and return expectations.

Disadvantages
- Downturns in the market can result in the account value being insufficient for expected charges.
- Agent requirements for NASD licensing are expensive to obtain and maintain.
- Administratively complex to manage one or many separate accounts for a single policy.
- SEC regulatory requirements added additional expenses to develop and maintain such policies.

### Secondary Guarantee Universal Life

Policies that terminate due to insufficient account value led to the development of riders and inherent guarantees within another variation of Universal Life. Secondary guarantee universal life products offer longer guarantees than level period term and a lower premium than whole life.

Advantages
- Prevent inadvertent termination due to insufficient account value.
- The length of the no-lapse guarantee can be structured with minimal premium requirements, allowing the product to be used as a death benefit focused design. A no-lapse guarantee provides an agreement by the insurance company to keep a permanent life insurance policy in force – even if the cash value in the policy drops to zero, or less than zero, provided that a specified minimum continuation premium is made at the required time.
- From the consumer’s perspective, they assume less risk, and the company assumes more risk.

Disadvantages
- Increases statutory reserve requirements.
- Use of shadow accounts may complicate administration and valuation processes.
- Minimum premium levels may be required for the no-lapse guarantee to be effective.

### Indexed Universal Life

The mechanics of an Indexed Universal Life (IUL) product are similar to a standard Universal Life (UL) with one exception. Both UL and IUL contracts may have a specified floor on the guaranteed minimum rate of return. The return created to the IUL is where the difference lies. The UL contract credit rate is determined from the return on invested assets supporting the policy minus the spread, investment expense, and default risk charges.  
  
The return on invested assets for an IUL contract is used as the budget to purchase options. The options produce an index straddle through the purchase of calls and puts. The return from the options establishes a credit to the return known as the cap.

Advantages
- Increased account value stability over traditional and variable universal life.
- Minimum guaranteed crediting interest.

Disadvantages
- Illustrated rate can be higher than the actual historical credited rate, leading to misleading projections.
- Complicated index formulas (e.g., annual point-to-point, monthly point-to-point).

### Annuities

Advantages
- Retirement savings
	- Annuities can be part of an investment or financial strategy to provide income in retirement.
- Tax deferral
	- All money contributed to annuities, regardless of the tax status of the annuity (that is, regardless of whether the premiums paid are qualified or non-qualified funds), grows tax-deferred until it is withdrawn from the annuity.
- No contribution limits
	- There is no limit on the amount that can be contributed to annuities, which can be an advantage of Individual Retirements Accounts (IRAs).
- Lifetime income
	- If a life-contingent payout option is chosen, payments are guaranteed to continue for the life of the annuitant. That is, the annuitant does not have to worry about outliving this source of retirement income.
- May avoid probate
	- Annuities are exempt from probate nationwide.

Disadvantages
- Long-term contract
	- An annuity is a long-term contract. Surrender charges generally apply if funds are withdrawn early (surrender charges often apply for the first 7 – 10 years and can be 10 percent or more of the annuity's value).
- Illiquid
	- In addition to incurring surrender charges for early withdrawal or surrender, a 10 percent penalty may apply if funds are withdrawn before age 59 ½.
- Complexity
	- Some types of annuities, such as fixed index annuities and variable annuities, may offer additional benefits and have additional charges or fees. In such cases, the contract can be more complex than the basic annuity described above.
- No capital gains
	- All withdrawals that are not returns or principal are taxed as ordinary income. With annuities, there is no opportunity for taxation as capital gains.

### Immediate Annuity

One in which annuity payments commence one payment mode (e.g., one month, if monthly payments are to be made; or one year, if payments will be made annually) after the annuity is purchased.

### Deferred Annuity

One in which annuity payments begin after a deferral period that is longer than the interval of an annuity payment; that is, if payments are to be made annually, a deferred annuity will have a deferral period of more than one year before such payments begin.

### Fixed Annuity

A fixed annuity guarantees at least a minimum rate of interest. This minimum guaranteed rate is set by the insurance company. Additionally, fixed annuities must provide a guaranteed minimum value that satisfies the standard nonforfeiture requirements applicable to fixed annuities. A fixed annuity can never lose money due to market or investment performance.
### Variable Annuity

By contrast, a variable annuity does not guarantee a minimum level of interest, nor is a variable annuity required to comply with standard nonforfeiture provisions that provide a guaranteed minimum value. A variable annuity can lose money based on investment performance.
### (Fixed) Indexed Annuity

An indexed annuity (also called a fixed index annuity or FIA) is a type of fixed annuity that determines the interest credited by reference to a market index, such as the S&P 500. Because an indexed annuity is a fixed annuity, it cannot lose value due to market performance and thus has a guaranteed interest rate of 0 percent. As with all fixed annuities, an indexed annuity must satisfy the minimum value requirements of standard nonforfeiture laws.

### Single Premium Annuity

Annuities can be funded via a lump sum called a single premium. The source of the lump sum could be savings, an inheritance, or a rollover from another product (such as another annuity, a 401(k) or an IRA). Some annuities require a lump sum single premium and will not accept additional premiums after issue.
### Flexible Premium Annuity

Annuities can also be funded via multiple premiums; an annuity that allows for multiple premiums is called a flexible premium annuity. Flexible premiums might be made via a regular salary reduction or via multiple transfers from other products (e.g., other annuities or IRAs) into the annuity.

### Liquidity Provisions

##### Partial Withdrawl
- A partial withdrawal provision allows for the withdrawal of a portion of the annuity’s accumulation value. An annuity might provide for a penalty-free partial withdrawal – that is, one that is not subject to surrender charges or other penalties – up to a state threshold, often 10 percent of the annuity’s accumulation value. Often, if more than the specified penalty-free withdrawal amount is taken, surrender charges or other penalties will apply to the excess.

##### Surrender
- A surrender applies to a full surrender of the contract for its current cash value or surrender value. Generally, during the surrender charge period, the cash value or surrender value will equal the full accumulation value of the annuity, less any applicable surrender charges, and less any other penalties or fees that apply upon early surrender (early surrender means a surrender before the end of the surrender charge period). After the surrender charge period, the annuity can be surrendered for its full accumulation value at any time.

### Other Features of Deferred Annuities

- Premium bonus
- Interest bonus
- Persisitency bonus
- Annuitization bonus
- Market Value Adjustment (MVA)
	- An MVA feature helps to mitigate disintermediation risk by sharing interest rate risk between the company and the policyholder. An MVA will generally increase the surrender value if surrender occurs at a time when interest rates are generally lower than they were at the time the contract was issued; conversely, an MVA will generally decrease the surrender value if surrender occurs at a time when interest rates are generally higher than they were when the contract was issued.
- Return of Premium (ROP)
	- An ROP provides guarantees that the surrender value will be no less than a return of net premiums. Net premiums are equal to all premiums paid for the annuity, less any withdrawals previously taken. The ROP might specify that this guaranteed amount is only available after a specified waiting period; e.g., the ROP might only apply after the 2nd contract anniversary.
- Bailout provisions
	- A bailout provision provides for full surrender of the contract, with any surrender charges or other penalties, if certain conditions are met. For example, a bailout may apply if the credited rate on the annuity drops below a stated threshold or if a specified market rate rises above a stated threshold.

### Annuities: Liquid Provisions - Annuitization

The third way to access the value of the annuity is annuitization.  
  
Annuitization is the provision by which a deferred annuity is converted into a stream of annuity payments. This occurs through the election of a payout option, such as a period-certain payout or a life-contingent payout. The payments are calculated based on the surrender value of the annuity and the payout option selected. In certain circumstances, a contract might calculate the annuity payments based on the full accumulation value of the annuity or after application of an annuitization bonus.

### Annuities: FIA Interest Crediting Methods

##### Participation Rate

A participation rate specifies the percentage of any index gain that is credited to the annuity. Assume the applicable participation rate is 50 percent. The participation rate is multiplied by the index gain to determine the interest credited to the annuity:  
  
50% participation rate x 7% index gain=3.5% interest credited to the annuity
##### Annual Cap Rate

An annual cap rate is an upper limit on the index gain that will be credited to the annuity. Assume the annual cap rate is 5 percent. This means that a maximum of 5% will be credited to the FIA in any given contract year. The interest credited to the annuity is calculated as  
  
Min(index gain, index cap rate). In this example, the interest credited to the annuity is Min(index gain of 7%, index cap rate of 5%) = 5 percent.
##### Annual Spreads

An annual spread is a specified amount that is subtracted from any index gain before determining the final interest credited to the annuity. Assume the annual spread is 1 percent. The spread is subtracted from the index gain to determine the interest credited to the annuity. In this example:  
  
index gain 7% - annual spread 1% = 6% interest credited to the annuity.  
  
It is important to note that the interest credited to the annuity can never be less than 0%. In this example, with an annual spread of 1 percent, this means that if the index gain is less than 1 percent, the interest credit will be 0 percent.  
  
Remember, the interest credited to the annuity can never be less than 0%, regardless of the performance of the underlying index. If the underlying index has negative performance, the interest credited to the annuity will be 0%.

### Annuities: FIA Guarantees

As noted earlier, an FIA is guaranteed a minimum interest rate of 0%, regardless of index performance. Additionally, state regulations generally also require that an FIA provide a guaranteed minimum surrender value. This value is defined by the Standard Nonforfeiture Law for Individual Deferred Annuities (SNFLIDA).  
  
While each state adopts its own version of the SNFLIDA, many states adopt a law that is substantially similar to the NAIC Model Regulation. This model regulation provides, in part, that the surrender value of an FIA will never be less than 87.5 percent of all premiums, less withdrawals, accumulated at a minimum interest rate. The minimum interest rate is calculated according to the terms set forth in the SNFLIDA.
